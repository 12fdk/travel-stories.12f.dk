# Spreadsheet vs App for Trip Planning: Which Should You Use?

The spreadsheet vs app for trip planning question is easier than it looks. The spreadsheet handles the grid well: one row per day, time blocks, a Money column, formulas that total the trip, and a link that shares the whole file with a travel companion.

What decides the answer is everything the grid can't hold: reminders, the plan edited on a phone, a packing list, the trip's photos, and the offline access a trip needs. Below is the verdict, then what the sheet is good at, where it breaks, the same criteria for five tools, and who should pick which.

## The answer: the grid in a sheet, the trip in an app

A spreadsheet is the right tool when the trip is bookings-light: you plan on a laptop, the day-by-day grid is the whole trip, and the budget math in formulas is the extra value. For that trip, a spreadsheet does the grid's job at no cost, and you already know how to use one.

A trip app takes over when the plan has to send reminders, hold the bookings, carry a packing list and work offline on a phone. Of the five tools in the table below, the spreadsheet covers the grid, TripIt and Wanderlog cover parts of the trip around it, and an all-in-one app like Travel Stories covers the whole plan. Which one fits is a short list at the end of this post.

## What the spreadsheet is good at

A spreadsheet earns its keep on the grid, and on the math.

- **The day-by-day grid.** One row per day, Morning, Afternoon and Evening blocks, plus Money and Notes. The [travel itinerary template](/blog/travel-itinerary-template/) is built exactly for a sheet: import the CSV into Google Sheets and the days become rows.
- **Budget math.** Formulas do the summing: a daily pace that totals against the trip cap, category columns, and no recounting of receipts at the end. Keep the categories in one place with the [travel budget template](/blog/travel-budget-template/).
- **Free and portable.** Google Sheets is free with a Google account (checked 27 Nov 2026). The file exports to CSV, opens in Excel and Numbers, and prints to paper for the person who plans with a pen.
- **Sharing.** One edit link shares the whole plan. A travel companion can open it from any browser, and the plan is the same file for both of you.

## Where the spreadsheet breaks once the trip starts

The sheet was built for a table on a desk. The trip happens on a phone, on the move, with no desk.

- **No reminders.** A sheet does not send you anything. Departure times, booking reminders and task due dates live in your head or in a second calendar, and the calendar is a third app you'd have to open.
- **Phone editing is a fight.** Google's own help says the Sheets app can create, view and edit files you've marked available offline, but you turn that on while you're online, before the trip ([Google's offline guide](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/6388102), checked 27 Nov 2026). And typing a changed plan into a grid with two thumbs is slower than deciding, so the plan you carry is the one from before the delay.
- **Nothing around the grid.** The packing list lives in a second file. The confirmations live in the email. The photos live in the camera roll. The plan is the grid, and the trip is everything else.
- **Mid-trip drift.** Two people editing the same grid from a hotel and a cafe. The sync handles the cells, not the decisions: who cut the morning, and when does the evening slot fill?

## When a day collapses: the check, and what each tool absorbs

A r/JapanTravel post is titled "10 Day Itinerary Expectation vs Reality from a Chronic Over-planner", another asks, of a Tokyo and Kyoto draft, "is this a slightly relaxed, sensible route?", and a third shares a finished 16-day itinerary, titled "Just came back from a 16 Day trip and this is what I did (itinerary)". The check the route question asks for is a count, and it runs in a minute, in a sheet or in an app:

1. Any day with more than one main plan in a half-day.
2. Any day with more than one evening plan.
3. Day 1 or the last day with more than a transfer and a short walk.
4. Travel time missing between two places in the same half-day.
5. Two days scattered across the map when one area would cover both.

When a flagged day collapses mid-trip, the tool decides what the fix costs. In the sheet, the changed plan is typed into a grid on a small screen, and the buffer is an empty cell you have to fill by hand. The app side is easier: the itinerary, the bookings and the packing list are already on the phone, so a changed plan is edited in the same place, offline.

Long trips need more buffer than short ones, because there are more days for a delay to arrive: group the days by area, keep one big thing per day, and let the evenings stay open. The rule behind the count is one main plan per half-day, and the travel itinerary template above shows it worked over five days.

## Who should pick which

- **Stay in the spreadsheet** for one short trip, planned on a laptop, with no bookings to juggle. The grid is the whole trip, the sheet is free, and nothing above says you need more.
- **Stay in Notion** if you already live there and want the plan as a database with your notes. Download the pages for offline use before you fly (Notion's pricing page lists offline pages on the free plan, checked 27 Nov 2026).
- **Pick TripIt** if the trip is flights and you want the confirmation emails collected into an itinerary automatically, with calendar sync on the free tier and flight and gate alerts on Pro ($49/year, US, checked 27 Nov 2026).
- **Pick Wanderlog** if you plan on a map, add road-trip stops, or plan with friends in real time. The free app covers the map and collaboration; Pro lists at $31.99 to $59.99 per year in the US App Store (checked 27 Nov 2026).
- **Pick Travel Stories** if the grid, the bookings, the budget, the packing list and the trip photos all live on one iPhone, offline and with no account. It is free for your first trip, and the Premium Lifetime upgrade (unlimited trips, spending charts, Apple Calendar export, sharing) is about $1.99 USD (19 DKK) once, with no subscription after it. [Get Travel Stories on the App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6756801168?ct=blog-spreadsheet-vs-app-trip-planning&mt=8).

For the grid, the sheet wins on cost, and for the trip itself, the app wins on structure. A free spreadsheet only stays cheaper if you do the rest by hand: the reminders in a second calendar, and the packing list in a note.
