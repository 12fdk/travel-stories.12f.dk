## What a trip budget app has to do

A trip budget app is useful when it keeps one number against your total and updates it as you spend, so you know where the money stands at the moment you decide to buy. It tracks what you spend, with a category, so after the trip you can see where the money actually went.

It works offline, because the spending happens in a market, on a train, or at a counter. Its price has to fit how often you travel, which matters more than any feature list for someone who takes two trips a year.

If your budget lives in a file you only open at home, you won't see it while you're spending. The app's job is to put that number in front of you when you're about to buy.

## What a good trip budget app needs

Look for four things. Without the first three, you probably won't use the tool during the trip.

**One number against your total.** The app holds a planned total and keeps it visible as you spend. You don't need a chart for that, but the number has to be there at the moment you are deciding. If you can only see the budget from a laptop at home, it can't help you decide while you're away.

**Actuals with a category.** "Actual" is only useful when it's entered the day it happens, in the right category. Skipping the entry turns the total into a guess and the category into a memory.

**Offline, no account.** The moment you spend is the moment you are away from wifi, in a market, on a train, at a counter. The app has to work there. It also helps if your data stays on the phone and doesn't need a login, so there's no sign-in to get stuck on when you have no signal.

**A price that fits your travel.** The feature you use most should be free to have. The question is what the paid tier is for, and whether you'd use it. If you travel a few times a year, a one-time price beats a subscription that bills you for the months you're not on a trip.

### Keeping food spending down

A reader on Reddit's r/Shoestring asked, "How do you keep food spending down without missing out on local food?" Numbers help here. Our [budget guide](/blog/how-to-budget-for-a-trip/) treats food at roughly 20% of the total as a rule of thumb.

On an illustrative $2,400 trip that is $480, or about $48 a day over ten days. At that pace you can eat market breakfasts, a casual lunch, and one sit-down dinner a day. Add one special meal you want to remember as a planned item in that share, and track food against the pace each evening. If a day runs over, the remaining days take a lower pace. The local food survives because you budgeted for it as one planned item.

## What you don't need

A few features look good in a screenshot and do nothing for the trip.

**Receipt scanning.** Scanning a receipt with the camera is convenient at home and fiddly on the road, when the receipt is already in your pocket. It can sit behind a paid tier, so check the in-app purchase list before you download, and treat it as a reason to pay, not a reason to track.

**Bank and card syncing.** Syncing can show card spending automatically, but it misses cash, and it can mean sharing bank access with the app. If you want it, check what it costs and which banks it supports.

**A feature you use twice.** If you only need a paid feature on two trips a year, a subscription bills you for the months between them. For occasional travel, compare the yearly cost with a one-time price.

What matters most is seeing your budget at the moment you decide to spend.

## What to check before you download

Five minutes in the App Store answers the big questions, whatever the screenshots say.

- **Scroll to In-App Purchases.** It shows what the app actually charges for. If the budget or the spending charts are a per-month or per-year item, that is a subscription; a single fixed price is a one-time unlock.
- **Test offline.** Put the phone in airplane mode and open the budget and the diary. If they load, the app works on the road. If it asks for a connection, you just learned what the free plan can't do.
- **Check the currency.** The app should let you track spending in the currency you think in, not force you to convert by hand.
- **Read the trial fine print.** Some free trials need a card and convert to a paid plan on their own. Note the renewal date, and check whether cancelling early ends the trial straight away.

The subscription question, with the trial traps named out, is in our guide to finding a [trip planner app with no subscription](/blog/trip-planner-app-no-subscription/).

## Who should pick what

If you travel once or twice a year and want the least to maintain, use the budget method with a daily log. The free CSV template in our [travel budget template](/blog/travel-budget-template/) post gives you the Planned, Actual, and Left columns, and it runs in any notes app or spreadsheet. You don't need to buy anything.

If you want the budget and the itinerary in one place and you travel on iPhone, the app above fits: one tool that holds the plan, the packing list, and the money, offline, and a price you pay once.

If you mostly need the money handled, skip the trip app and use a spreadsheet or a dedicated expense app. The itinerary is not the job you're doing, so don't pay for features around it. And if you are on Android, budgeting with a group you need to split costs with, or syncing a bank account, the iPhone-only app is not the fit, so a spreadsheet or a dedicated expense app will suit you better.
