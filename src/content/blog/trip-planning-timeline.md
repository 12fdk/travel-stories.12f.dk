There is a right answer to "how far in advance should I plan a trip," and it is not a number: it is the length of your slowest step. A flight you can change for free can be booked yesterday; a passport or visa that takes two months to arrive cannot be started next week. A trip planning timeline works by starting from the deadlines that cannot be compressed — documents, other people's time off, seasonal peaks — and working backwards. For a typical flight-based international trip, that backwards count lands at roughly three months. For a domestic weekend, two to four weeks. Everything below is the sequence that works for the common case, with the places where you can skip ahead.

## How far in advance should I plan a trip?

The general rule: **three months for a flight-based trip, two to four weeks for a short domestic one, and longer if anything involves document processing.** Three things decide where your trip sits on that range:

1. **Documents.** A new US passport takes 4–6 weeks of routine processing plus up to two weeks of mailing each way, according to the [US State Department's current processing times](https://travel.state.gov/en/passports/apply/help/processing-time.html). If your passport is expired, missing, or close to expiring — some destination countries require it to remain valid for six months beyond your entry date — the passport, not the flight, is your first deadline. Visa processing times vary from days to months by country and nationality; check the embassy of the destination, not a blog.
2. **Other people's calendars.** If you are travelling with a group, the slowest step is often not any booking but finding a week that works for everyone. Telling your travel companions "we're tentatively doing X in [month]" three months out is not nagging; it is the whole planning timeline.
3. **Peak season.** Summer, major holidays, and festival season fill up accommodation and seats earlier than off-season does. A July week in a popular city is a three-months-out trip even if the trip itself is four nights.

If none of those apply — documents current, travelling alone, off-season dates — you can plan most of the trip in the final month and still be fine.

## The 3-2-1 trip planning timeline at a glance

| When | The job | The main output |
|---|---|---|
| **3 months out** | Documents and the two big decisions | Valid passport/visa, fixed dates, agreed budget |
| **2 months out** | The main bookings | Flights or transport to the destination, accommodation, insurance |
| **1 month out** | Logistics and fine-tuning | Day-by-day draft, pre-booked tickets, packing list, final budget |
| **2 weeks out** | Final checks | Confirmations re-verified, offline copies, the final [pre-departure checklist](/blog/travel-checklist-before-leaving/) |
| **Last days** | Execute | Pack, check in, go |

The table is the shape; the sections below are what each stage actually involves, and which parts you can skip if your trip is shorter.

## Three months out: documents and dates first

This stage exists because it is the only stage with deadlines that live outside your control.

**Check your documents.** Is your passport valid — and valid long enough for the specific country you are entering? Do you need a visa, and what does its processing actually take? Some destinations require an online pre-travel authorisation (the US has ESTA, the Schengen area is rolling out ETIAS); complete the application well before departure so an error or refusal is something you can still fix. Note your passport number, issue date, and expiry in one place now — you will type them into forms for weeks.

**Fix the dates.** This is the decision that unblocks everything else, and it is the one people drag out longest. The trick is to decide a *range* rather than an exact date if you are not sure: "the second half of June" gets you comparing the same prices and the same availability, and you narrow to exact days at the two-months-out mark.

**Agree the budget with the people you are travelling with.** If the total is going to be shared, say the number out loud now, per person, before anyone starts reserving. The [travel budget guide](/blog/how-to-budget-for-a-trip/) walks through splitting it into six categories with a buffer, and the [travel budget template](/blog/travel-budget-template/) is a ready-made sheet. The rule of thumb from that post still applies here: 10–15% of the total held as a buffer, unassigned.

**Start the itinerary skeleton.** Just the big frame — which cities, how many nights each, what trip you will drive or fly between them — not the day-by-day plan. That detailed pass belongs in the final month, once the bookings are fixed. If this is your first planning pass, the [step-by-step guide](/blog/how-to-plan-a-trip-step-by-step/) covers the full sequence.

## Two months out: the main bookings

The frame is fixed, so the expensive parts get booked now.

**Transport to the destination.** On most routes, prices settle in the one-to-three-months window and firm up in the final weeks, so this is the default moment to buy tickets rather than watch them. Buy flexibility where it is cheap: a fare class you can change is worth more than a discount you have to bet on. If you are driving instead — a road trip changes the whole timeline — book or reserve the overnight stops that are hard to change in peak season, and put a service check on the car now so a fault is a two-day fix, not a trip-ender.

**Accommodation.** The same window logic applies. Book the nights you cannot lose — the arrival night, the festival week, the remote place with one option — and leave a few nights flexible if the itinerary is still settling.

**Travel insurance.** Buy it soon after your first trip payment. Some policies only qualify for trip-cancellation cover if you buy within 14–21 days of the initial payment — the limit is set by the policy, so read yours — and buying it later leaves the exact window you most need it unprotected.

**Pre-book what sells out.** Popular attractions, a few key meals, and any tour that requires a booking weeks ahead. You are not planning every day yet; you are removing the things that would vanish by month one.

## One month out: logistics and fine-tuning

This is the stage where the plan becomes a day-by-day itinerary.

- **Build the day-by-day plan.** One activity per morning is a healthy pace; two or more per day is a spreadsheet that will be rewritten on day three. Leave the last day of each location deliberately loose.
- **Book the small things that need lead time:** airport transfers, train tickets for fixed legs, bike or car rentals, a museum pass that saves queues.
- **Write the packing list.** Start from a [ready-made international packing list](/blog/packing-list-for-international-travel/) and adapt it — the edits you make this month are the list you will reuse next trip.
- **Set the budget and its buffer.** The [budget template](/blog/travel-budget-template/) has the six categories and the 10–15% buffer ready; the numbers from your bookings drop straight in.
- **Re-verify every confirmation** — dates, names exactly as on your passport, booking references — and put a copy of each where you can reach it offline.

This is also the stage where a phone beats a notebook: the plan stops being a document and starts being something you open at 7 am at an airport. ## Two weeks out: the final checks

No new decisions from here on — only verification. Run through the [travel checklist before leaving](/blog/travel-checklist-before-leaving/): re-confirm the bookings, download or print the confirmations, check the destination's travel advisories, verify payment methods will work abroad, and do the phone-side setup (offline maps, the itinerary available without a connection). If anything changes in this window, it changes the plan, and a plan this close out should change as little as possible.

## Last minute: what actually has to happen before you board

Sometimes the timeline collapses — work ends early, a friend backs out of a long trip and you go anyway, or the trip simply started small. A genuinely last-minute trip works if you hold the plan to the minimum list:

1. **Documents are valid.** Passport, visa or pre-travel authorisation, insurance if you still need it. If any of these is missing or expired, the trip is a different trip — sort that first, before spending anything.
2. **Transport is booked for your remaining dates.** Not the cheapest; the ones that fit.
3. **You have somewhere to sleep** for the nights you have locked.
4. **You can get from the airport or station to where you are staying** — that one transfer, at least.
5. **The budget is still a number.** Even a rough one, because the buffer still exists and the buffer still has to cover the surprises that hit last-minute travellers hardest.

That is the whole floor. Everything else — the day-by-day plan, the pre-booked tickets — is upside you collect only if you have the time.

## The timeline by trip type

| Trip | Start planning | The long pole (what forces the schedule) |
|---|---|---|
| Short domestic trip | 2–4 weeks | Your own calendar and accommodation availability |
| Road trip | 2–4 weeks | Vehicle service and any remote overnight stops that sell out |
| Flight-based international trip | ~3 months | Documents, then bookings |
| Trip with a visa requirement | 3–6+ months | Visa processing — check the embassy, it varies by country |
| Group trip | 3+ months | Finding dates everyone can keep |
| Peak-season trip (summer, holidays) | ~3–4 months | Accommodation and seats filling early |

The pattern in the table is the whole method: find your long pole, check it today, and build the rest of the timeline backwards from there.
