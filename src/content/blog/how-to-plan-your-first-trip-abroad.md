## How to plan your first trip abroad: the short version

Work through eight steps in order. Check that your passport is valid and shortlist places you can enter. Pick a destination and dates. Set one total budget with a buffer. Test your route, aiming for at least 3 nights per base. Book the journey and your first and last nights. Sketch a loose plan for each day, sort insurance and money, and then pack.

This order protects you from paying for a plan you can't enter, afford or enjoy. Our general [nine-step method](/blog/how-to-plan-a-trip-step-by-step/) covers the wider process, and this version adds a pace test for first-timers. If your date is fixed by a wedding or a festival, start from that date and work through the rest in the same order.

## Step 1: Check your passport and shortlist where you can go

Start with the document that decides where you can go. Confirm your passport is valid and note its expiry date. Then write down two or three places you'd like to visit and check the entry rules for each one for your nationality. Some trips need a visa or permit that you apply for weeks ahead, while others need only a valid passport, so don't reuse a friend's answer.

The [Travel Documents Checklist](/blog/travel-documents-checklist/) explains the rules by destination type. For the rules themselves, go to your own government's travel advice. The US publishes its advice at [travel.state.gov](https://travel.state.gov/), and many other countries have an equivalent.

## Step 2: Pick a destination and dates

Choose from your shortlist. If you're nervous about the trip, favour somewhere with public transport you can work out and a language barrier you can manage. Then look up the weather for your month, and check it if the season is the reason you're going.

Traveling alone for the first time? Book your first and last nights somewhere easy to reach, and leave one unplanned day at the end. A change of mind then costs you little.

## Step 3: Set one total budget with a buffer

Set a single number for the whole trip before you book anything. [How to Budget for a Trip](/blog/how-to-budget-for-a-trip/) splits it into transport, stay, food, activities, local transit and a buffer. Add 10-15% on top and don't assign it to anything. It's there for surprises such as a checked-bag fee or a missed train.

Use real prices for your dates, because a rough daily figure hides the costs you haven't looked up. Every later decision gets checked against this total.

## Step 4: Test your route for pace

Reddit's first-timers keep asking whether a route is too packed, and you can test yours with numbers you already have. These rules of thumb are our starting point, and you can break them for good reason:

- **At least 3 nights per base.** With fewer, you spend more of the trip packing and checking in.
- **About 4 bases at most in two weeks.** Each extra base adds a move.
- **Count each transfer as a half-day.** A long train or a flight, with a hotel on either side, takes up much of a day.

Now divide your nights by your bases.

**A worked example.** One itinerary posted on Reddit covered 14 nights across five bases: Tokyo, Kyoto, Miyajima, Osaka and Kawaguchi. That's 2.8 nights per base. Five bases mean four moves, so about 2 days go to transit.

The route isn't impossible, but it misses the 3-night guide. Drop one base and share out its nights, and you'll have 3.5 nights per base and one move fewer. Ask yourself which move you can cut, and leave the question of the best city for later.

**A longer trip.** Length alone doesn't make a route packed. Seventeen days over 4 bases with one main activity a day leaves room to spare. Seventeen days over 6 bases with two main activities a day gives you about 2.8 nights per base and much less room.

If every base has 3 or more nights and you can name the days lost to transfers, your route has room for delays. If it doesn't, cut a base before you pay for the middle of the trip.

When you choose where to stay, one well-connected base in each city saves you daily commuting and repeat check-ins. You can narrow down the neighbourhood once your route is settled.

## Step 5: Book the journey and your first and last nights

Book the journey first, then your first and last nights. These bookings fix your dates and give you a place to land and leave from. Fill in the middle after that, as long as your budget still holds.

Before you pay, read the cancellation terms in the offer. They vary by airline, hotel and country, and they decide what you can get back if the plan changes. A cheap fare that can't be changed may still be right for you, as long as you've chosen it knowingly. On a first trip, compare the price difference with what you'd lose if your dates moved.

## Step 6: Sketch a loose plan for each day

Now write the day-by-day plan. Put in the fixed items first, including flights, check-ins and timed tickets, with their exact times. Then add one main activity per day and leave the rest open. If you're crossing several time zones, keep your first day light.

## Step 7: Sort insurance and money

Choose travel insurance that covers what would hurt most to pay for yourself, such as a medical visit, a lost bag or a cancelled trip. Read the policy's exclusions before you buy. Then decide how much cash you'll carry and check whether cards are widely accepted where you're going. [How Much Cash to Bring on Vacation](/blog/how-much-cash-to-bring-on-vacation/) walks through it.

## Step 8: Pack and run the last-week checks

Start your packing list a week or more before you leave, because a list is more reliable than your memory. In the final days, confirm your bookings, check your passport and documents again, and make sure your money and insurance are sorted.

## I've already booked and I'm panicking

Reddit has plenty of posts from people who've booked a trip and now feel overwhelmed. Sometimes life has changed, and cancelling is the right call. Before you decide, this takes about five minutes:

1. Read the cancellation terms in your confirmations, so you know what you'd get back.
2. Cut one base, and move its nights to a base you like.
3. Remove one more transfer, or leave a free day at the end.
4. Run the pace test from step 4 again.

If the new plan feels manageable, you may have found the problem. If you still want out, you can decide knowing the actual cost.

## Three things to do today

- **Check your passport** and the entry rules for the places on your shortlist.
- **Run the pace test** on the route you have in mind: divide nights by bases, then count each transfer as a half-day.
- **Set your total budget** with a 10-15% buffer, and write down what you'll book first.
